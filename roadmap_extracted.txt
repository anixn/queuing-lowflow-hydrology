1. Executive Summary
This document locates the research junction between queueing theory and hydrological drought in the
publication record of Ankit Deshmukh, and turns that junction into an executable three-year research
plan. It responds to a specific request: screen seven candidate research topics that apply queueing theory
to computational hydrology, select the three most useful for low-flow, drought and climate-change
research in water resources, and lay out a roadmap with data, methods, publications and milestones. The
document is written as a personal research plan: it is self-critical about risks, explicit about skill gaps, and
disciplined about what will and will not be claimed as novelty.
The junction itself is easy to state and surprisingly unclaimed. A drought is, at its core, an accumulation
of unmet water demand while supply operates as a finite, weather-driven service process; queueing theory
is exactly the mathematics of arrivals, service capacity, congestion and waiting. The author has published
on both sides of this divide. The hydrology-and-drought side includes two Water Resources Research
papers on catchment vulnerability and climate-change behaviour (Deshmukh and Singh, 2016; 2019), a
2025 IEEE conference framework that uses meteorological drought indicators as proxies for hydrological
drought, the Drought Intelligence Explorer - a 45-year, district-level Indian dataset of ten drought
indicators - an ICSSR-funded early-warning project for urban floods and agricultural droughts, and an
in-development R package for hydrological analysis that already computes drought indices, baseflow
separation and trend statistics. The queueing side is the co-authored Varshney et al. (2025) paper in
Scientific Reports, which analyses electric-vehicle charging stations with a finite-population, multi-server
continuous-time Markov chain including state-dependent service thresholds, customer balking, reneging
and feedback.

Two facts make this junction historically legitimate rather than merely fashionable. First, the
mathematical theory of dams and storage - Moran (1954), Gani (1957) and Prabhu’s monograph on
stochastic storage processes - was explicitly built as a cousin of queueing theory: inflows, releases and
capacity limits map one-to-one onto arrivals, service and buffer size. Second, the two fields then
diverged: hydrology moved toward conceptual rainfall-runoff modelling and standardized indices, while
operations research refined the stochastic machinery. Very little modern work has brought the machinery
back to drought science; the rare exceptions include Unami and colleagues’ stochastic control of
rainwater-harvesting systems (Unami et al., 2015). Reclaiming storage-as-queue for drought research,
with modern data and machine learning, is the organising idea of this plan.

(machine-learning-assisted non-stationary queueing for hydrological drought risk) is the methodological

2. The Junction: Where Queueing Meets
Hydrological Drought
A research junction is only credible if both columns of the intersection can be evidenced from the record.
This section audits the two columns of the author’s portfolio - drought-oriented computational hydrology
and stochastic queueing analysis - then anchors the connection in the classical storage-theory literature
and states the specific gap that the proposed programme fills. One caveat applies throughout: the Google
Scholar profile was behind an automated-traffic block at the time of writing, so the publication record
was compiled from ankitdeshmukh.com, ResearchGate and indexed search results, all accessed in
November 2026.
2.1 The hydrology-and-drought column
The drought-oriented work has two defining traits that matter for the junction. The first trait is a
comparative, catchment-to-region mindset. The Water Resources Research paper on physio-climatic
controls of watershed vulnerability (Deshmukh and Singh, 2016) combined exploratory modelling with
comparative hydrology to quantify how climate and land-use change propagate into water availability
across 69 United States catchments. Its 2019 successor used Whittaker biome classification as a transfer
framework, showing that allowing catchment parameters to change with biome-shifted climates
significantly alters estimates of future catchment behaviour (Deshmukh and Singh, 2019). Both papers
are, at heart, studies of how catchments buffer and transmit variability - precisely the property a queueing
formulation abstracts into buffer size and service capacity.
The second trait is applied drought monitoring infrastructure. The 2025 conference paper with Najwani
and Kumari builds a sustainable framework in which meteorological drought indicators serve as proxies
for hydrological drought, an explicit exercise in connecting deficit signals across compartments. The
Drought Intelligence Explorer scales this to a public platform: 45 years of data, all-India district coverage,
ten drought indicators including SPI, SPEI, soil-moisture anomaly and vegetation condition, runs-based
event catalogues, Mann-Kendall and Sen trend tests, and lagged-correlation propagation analysis with
bootstrap confidence intervals. The ICSSR-funded longitudinal study adds the community side - risk
perception and preparedness for urban floods and agricultural droughts - which later becomes the

Seen together, the column’s shared intellectual object is the temporal bookkeeping of water shortage:
deficits accumulate, persist and recover. What the column lacks is a mechanism-level representation of
that bookkeeping. Standardized indices describe anomalies relative to climatology; they do not ask which
part of the deficit came from arrival pressure, which from constrained service, or how long the system
needs to clear a backlog once supply recovers. That question is structural, and structural questions are
what queueing theory answers natively.
2.2 The queueing column
The Varshney et al. (2025) Scientific Reports paper - with the author fifth of nine - develops control
strategies for electric-vehicle charging stations using a finite-population, multi-server queueing model
solved as a continuous-time Markov chain. The formulation is richer than a textbook M/M/c: service
providers switch to a faster rate when congestion crosses a threshold; arriving customers balk with a
state-dependent probability when the queue looks hopeless; waiting customers renege at an impatience
rate; and dissatisfied customers feed back into the system with probability ξ. Chapman-Kolmogorov
dynamics yield the full state distribution, from which waiting times, queue lengths, utilisation and
service-quality metrics are computed. The paper had accumulated roughly 29 citations within months of
publication, as reported by indexed search results in November 2026 - evidence that the applied
stochastic-modelling toolkit travels well beyond operations research.

2.3 The historical bridge: storage theory was queueing theory
The junction also has deep roots. Moran (1954) formulated the probability theory of dams and storage
systems, modelling inflows as random inputs to a finite reservoir with controlled releases - structurally a
queue with state-dependent service. Gani (1957) made the analogy explicit, solving storage problems with
the queue-theoretic machinery of the day, and Prabhu’s monograph (1998) unified queues, insurance risk
and dams as one subject: stochastic storage processes. Hydrology, however, took a different path after the
1960s - conceptual rainfall-runoff models, then standardized indices, then large distributed models -
while the stochastic storage line survived mainly in operations research. Modern attempts to bridge the
two are rare and small-scale; the clearest recent example is the stochastic modelling and control of
rainwater-harvesting systems for irrigation during dry spells (Unami et al., 2015), which treats a
tank-irrigation system as a stochastic input-output-storage problem. The bridge exists in principle, is
dormant in practice, and has never been operationalised with today’s data streams, machine learning, or at
drought-relevant spatial scales.
2.4 The gap, stated precisely
Combining the two columns and the historical anchor yields four concrete gaps. First, drought indices
standardize anomalies but do not represent deficit accumulation as a service-process outcome, so
persistence and recovery time - the quantities managers actually care about - remain descriptive statistics
rather than model outputs. Second, machine learning in drought research is overwhelmingly predictive:
models forecast an index, but do not parameterise an interpretable stochastic process, which limits both
physical insight and extrapolation under climate change. Third, the queueing view of water systems exists
only in isolated niches (small tanks, harvesting systems); no one has built non-stationary, multi-class
queue frameworks for hydrological drought, low flows, agricultural deficits or groundwater backlogs at
district-to-basin scales. Fourth, the author is unusually positioned to close this: the queueing mechanics
are already published, the drought data infrastructure is already built, and the comparative-hydrology
training enforces the multi-basin discipline that keeps such a framework honest. Table 2 summarises how
each existing asset converts into a capability the programme needs.

3.1 Priority 1 - Topic 6: machine-learning-assisted non-stationary
queueing for hydrological drought risk
Conventional hydrological drought analysis characterises propagation from precipitation deficits through
catchment storage to streamflow using standardized indicators and statistical lag relationships. These tools
describe when drought occurs but not the accumulation dynamics of unmet demand under a time-varying,
capacity-constrained supply - and they presume statistical stability that a warming, intensively managed
world no longer guarantees. A queueing reformulation treats water demand events as arrivals at rate λ(t)
and streamflow-mediated supply - especially baseflow, the dry-season workhorse of rivers - as a service
process at rate μ(t), both explicitly time-varying and both estimable by machine learning from
hydroclimatic and geospatial predictors. The queue state - backlog, waiting time, utilisation, recovery
time - then becomes a physically interpretable drought-risk object rather than a black-box score.
Four research questions structure the work. First, can arrival and service intensity functions λ(t) and μ(t)
be estimated with quantified uncertainty for gauged catchments, using gradient-boosted trees and
recurrent networks trained on hydroclimatic predictors, and validated against the observed flow regime?
Second, do queue-derived indicators - expected backlog, probability that the backlog exceeds a critical
duration, and queue-clearing time - add information about drought persistence and recovery beyond
established indices such as the standardized streamflow index and low-flow duration statistics? Third,
how do catchment characteristics - storage, geology, land cover, climate - modulate queue behaviour,
extending the comparative-hydrology programme of the WRR 2016 and 2019 papers into a
stochastic-process setting? Fourth, under projected climate non-stationarity, how do utilisation ratios
approach unity and how do backlog distributions shift - the queueing signature of a catchment crossing

The method sketch proceeds in three stages. Stage one builds the physical queue definition for a stream
reach: arrivals are period-wise water demand pressure (sectoral withdrawal requirement aggregated over
the contributing area), the server is the flow-regime-mediated supply capacity referenced to a low-flow
threshold such as the 95th-percentile exceedance flow, the buffer is reach storage, and reneging
represents demand abandonment when deficits persist beyond crop or ecosystem tolerance. Stage two
estimates λ(t) and μ(t) by supervised learning, with careful separation of training, validation and
drought-test periods, and uncertainty by quantile-regression variants or conformal methods. Stage three
solves the resulting non-stationary queue - analytically for tractable birth-death cases, by discrete-event
simulation otherwise - and derives indicators, which are then benchmarked against SSI-class indices on
independent catchments and drought events using pre-specified metrics, never correlation alone.
The first experiment is deliberately modest: ten reference basins from the United States GAGES-II
population - chosen to overlap the basins analysed in the WRR 2016 work - and ten Indian basins from
the CWC network, simulating Mt/Mt/1 dynamics with known λ(t), μ(t) functions, verifying the solver
against discrete-event simulation, and testing whether queue state recovers known drought episodes from
the historical record. The main risks are demand-side data scarcity in Indian basins, mitigated by
irrigation-requirement proxies and sensitivity analysis, and identifiability of jointly estimated λ and μ,
mitigated by structural constraints that tie μ(t) to observed flow and λ(t) to observed demand covariates.
Target output: Paper P3, submitted by the end of 2028, with the methods library released in the R
package.
3.2 Priority 2 - Topic 1: queueing-based agricultural drought
characterisation through accumulated water deficits
Agricultural drought is currently diagnosed through meteorological ancestry - rainfall anomalies,
soil-moisture departures, vegetation stress - none of which represents the accumulation of unmet crop
water demand under constrained supply. Yet that accumulation is what determines yield loss, and
irrigation availability is what moderates it: two districts with identical rainfall deficits can have entirely
different agricultural outcomes if one has reliable canal service and the other has none. A queueing
framework makes this explicit: crop water demand arrives on a seasonal and weather-driven schedule,
rain and irrigation deliveries act as service processes with finite capacity, and the integral of unmet
demand - the queue backlog over time and area - is the agricultural drought measure. Persistence and
recovery follow from queue dynamics rather than from ad hoc lag fitting.
The research questions are three. First, does the backlog-based indicator reproduce known historical
agricultural droughts across Indian districts - which the Drought Intelligence Explorer’s 45-year panel can
test directly against its own SMA and VCI records - and with what lead or lag? Second, how do supply
constraints alter deficit accumulation between districts with contrasting irrigation development, for
example canal-irrigated districts of Punjab against rain-fed districts of Maharashtra’s semi-arid interior?
Third, how sensitive are conclusions to the demand model, estimated from crop coefficients and cropping

3.3 Priority 3 - Topic 7: groundwater demand and recharge
queueing under prolonged drought
Groundwater is the slow queue of the water system: recharge arrives episodically, extraction requests
arrive continuously through pumping decisions, and the aquifer serves both while its own capacity to
deliver degrades as heads decline. Conventional assessment tracks storage change and pumping quantities
but not the accumulation of unmet demand under extraction constraints - the backlog that manifests as
failed borewells, deepening wells and crop stress in exactly the multi-year droughts that climate change is
expected to prolong. A state-dependent queueing model fits naturally: extraction demand as the arrival
process, sustainable yield as a service process whose rate degrades with storage state, buffer as aquifer
storage, and the EV paper’s state-dependent service-threshold machinery mapping directly onto pumping
restrictions that tighten as levels fall.
Three questions guide the extension. First, what is the distribution of the extraction backlog and of
restriction duration under observed recharge regimes - in steady state, and under non-stationary recharge
projected for the region? Second, how do alternative restriction policies - the thresholds at which service
throttles - trade off demand backlog against aquifer depletion, and where are the policy-relevant
thresholds in state-space? Third, can independent gravimetric observations validate the queue state: do
GRACE and GRACE-FO terrestrial-water-storage anomalies cohere with modelled storage trajectories
in the study districts, given their coarse spatial footprint? The modelling couples the queue to a simple
water-balance storage module rather than a full groundwater model, which keeps the framework
interpretable and the data requirements realistic; the queue contributes the demand-backlog accounting
that the balance model lacks.
The first experiment selects two or three districts with usable CGWB observation-well records, driving
records and GRACE coverage, calibrates the coupled queue-balance model on the pre-2015 record, and
tests whether modelled backlog states correspond to observed level anomalies and reported restriction

3.4 Why the remaining four topics wait
The deferred topics remain on the shelf with explicit re-entry conditions. Spatially non-stationary
queueing for runoff generation and hydrological connectivity (candidate 2) is the deepest science among
the seven, but it presupposes exactly the non-stationary machinery that Topic 6 develops; it re-enters as a
grant-scale project once P3 is published. Irrigation scheduling and reservoir multi-sector allocation
(candidates 3 and 4) are operational allocation queues rather than physical-process queues - valuable,
fundable, and deliberately sequenced after the science core, because the methodological guardrails
distinguish the two and the programme must not blur them in its first papers. Urban drainage congestion
(candidate 5) requires coupling to hydraulic routing, a specialism outside the current portfolio, and is best
pursued through a collaboration once the framework papers establish the approach. None of the four is
abandoned; all four are downstream of the core in a dependency sense, and the funding proposal in Year
2 explicitly frames them as the second tranche.

The computational toolchain is equally deliberate. The queue mathematics is implemented twice by
design: once as exact CTMC solvers in Python (NumPy and SciPy linear algebra over birth-death
generators, extending the formulations of the Varshney et al. paper) and once as discrete-event simulation
in simpy, so that every analytical result has an independent numerical check. Machine-learning
components use PyTorch for recurrent estimators and XGBoost for tabular gradient boosting, with
conformal or quantile variants for prediction uncertainty. Geospatial pipelines use Google Earth Engine,
xarray and geopandas, matching the GeoHydroLab infrastructure. The hydrological side lives in the
in-development R package, which provides baseflow separation, drought indices and trend tests, and
which becomes the distribution vehicle for queue-derived indicators in Year 3. Everything is
version-controlled with pinned environments and unit tests, a practice the package project has already
adopted; compute needs are modest - a university workstation or a cloud notebook tier suffices, since the
heavy lifting is estimation on tabular and raster data rather than large-model training.

6.1 Year 1 (2027) - Foundations
The first half-year builds the queueing core library: birth-death CTMC solvers for single-server and
multi-server queues with time-varying rates, finite capacity, reneging and state-dependent service
thresholds, ported from the mathematics of the Varshney et al. (2025) paper and rewritten for water
processes. A synthetic benchmark suite accompanies it - known λ(t) and μ(t) functions with closed-form
or simulation-verified answers - so that every later application inherits validated components. In parallel,
writing begins on P1, the positioning review that reclaims the storage-theory lineage, and the Topic 1 data
pipeline assembles district-level demand and supply series from the Drought Intelligence Explorer’s
stack. The year closes with P1 submitted (Q3) and the Topic 1 pilot running on two contrasting states.
6.2 Year 2 (2028) - Applications
The second year converts the core into applications. The Topic 1 study completes and submits as P2 in
Q2 2028. The Topic 6 machine-learning machinery - recurrent and gradient-boosted estimators for λ(t)
and μ(t) with uncertainty quantification - comes online in the first half, then runs the low-flow case
studies across the Indian and United States basin sets in the second half, closing the year with the P3 draft

The skill gaps are three, each with a closing action. Stochastic-process depth beyond the published
CTMC work - renewal arguments, fluid limits, heavy-traffic intuitions - is addressed by a structured
self-study of Prabhu (1998) and standard queueing texts during the first half of 2027, timed to feed
directly into P1’s scholarship. Machine-learning engineering at production standard - conformal
uncertainty, model monitoring, reproducible pipelines - is addressed through the package project’s
existing test-and-release discipline, extended to the estimation components. Grant writing is addressed by
pairing with senior colleagues who have served on review panels, using the Year 1 artefacts as the
evidence base rather than writing on promises.
The methodological guardrails, adopted as pre-commitments for every paper in the programme, are as
follows. Do not automatically assume Poisson arrivals, exponential service or stationarity; test them and
report departures. Account for temporal dependence and spatial autocorrelation in both estimation and
validation, since hydrological series violate independence casually. Compare queue-derived indicators
against established drought indices under pre-specified metrics, on held-out events and basins, and
demonstrate incremental information through ablation rather than asserting superiority. Quantify and
report uncertainty in estimated arrival and service processes, since an indicator without error bars is not
usable by managers. Distinguish physically based hydrological queueing from operational allocation
queueing, and keep the two strands visibly separate in publications. And never claim a new index is
superior on the basis of correlation with existing indices alone - the weakest possible evidence, and the
most common failure in the indicator literature. These commitments are written into the design sections
of P2 and P3 before any experiments run.
